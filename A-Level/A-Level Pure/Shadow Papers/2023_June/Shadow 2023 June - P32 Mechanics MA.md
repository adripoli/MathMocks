@q 1
@topic Constant acceleration from rest
@marks 3
@question
(a) The car starts from rest, so $u = 0$, with $a = 2.8$ and $t = 6$ \\
Use $v = u + at$: \\
$v = 0 + 2.8 \times 6$ \\
$v = 16.8$ m s$^{-1}$ \\
(b) Use $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}$: \\
$s = 0 \times 6 + \frac{1}{2} \times 2.8 \times 6^{2}$ \\
$6^{2} = 36$ \\
$\frac{1}{2} \times 2.8 = 1.4$ \\
$s = 1.4 \times 36$ \\
$s = 50.4$ m \\
(Check: average speed $\frac{0 + 16.8}{2} = 8.4$, and $8.4 \times 6 = 50.4$)

@q 2
@topic Particle pulled on rough plane
@marks 4
@question
(a) The plane is horizontal and $P$ does not move up or down, so the normal reaction balances the weight: \\
$R = 8g$ \\
$R = 8 \times 9.8$ \\
$R = 78.4$ N \\
(b) Equation of motion horizontally ($F = ma$): the 35 N force forwards, friction $F$ backwards \\
$35 - F = 8 \times 1.05$ \\
$8 \times 1.05 = 8.4$ \\
$35 - F = 8.4$ \\
Add $F$ and subtract 8.4: \\
$F = 35 - 8.4$ \\
$F = 26.6$ N \\
(c) The particle is moving, so friction is limiting: $F = \mu R$ \\
$26.6 = \mu \times 78.4$ \\
Divide by 78.4: \\
$\mu = \frac{26.6}{78.4}$ \\
$\mu = 0.3393$ \\
$\mu = 0.34$ to 2 significant figures

@q 3
@topic Velocity vector, North-West, acceleration
@marks 9
@question
(a) Put $t = 0$ into $\mathbf{v} = (7 - t - 2t^{2})\mathbf{i} + (t^{2} - t + 8)\mathbf{j}$ \\
$\mathbf{i}$ component: $7 - 0 - 0 = 7$ \\
$\mathbf{j}$ component: $0 - 0 + 8 = 8$ \\
$\mathbf{v} = 7\mathbf{i} + 8\mathbf{j}$ \\
Speed is the magnitude of the velocity: \\
$\text{speed} = \sqrt{7^{2} + 8^{2}}$ \\
$= \sqrt{49 + 64}$ \\
$= \sqrt{113}$ \\
$= 10.6$ m s$^{-1}$ \\
(b) North-West is between north and west, so the $\mathbf{i}$ (east) component is negative, the $\mathbf{j}$ (north) component is positive, and they are equal in size \\
$\mathbf{i}$ component $= -(\mathbf{j}$ component$)$ \\
$7 - t - 2t^{2} = -(t^{2} - t + 8)$ \\
Expand the bracket: \\
$7 - t - 2t^{2} = -t^{2} + t - 8$ \\
Add $2t^{2}$ to both sides, and subtract $7$ and add $t$: \\
$0 = t^{2} + 2t - 15$ \\
Factorise: \\
$(t + 5)(t - 3) = 0$ \\
$t = -5$ or $t = 3$ \\
$t \geq 0$, so reject $t = -5$ \\
$t = 3$ \\
(Check at $t = 3$: $\mathbf{i}$ component $7 - 3 - 18 = -14$ and $\mathbf{j}$ component $9 - 3 + 8 = 14$, so it is moving North-West) \\
(c) Acceleration is the derivative of velocity: \\
$7 - t - 2t^{2} \to -1 - 4t$ \\
$t^{2} - t + 8 \to 2t - 1$ \\
$\mathbf{a} = (-1 - 4t)\mathbf{i} + (2t - 1)\mathbf{j}$ m s$^{-2}$ \\
(d) The acceleration is parallel to $\mathbf{i}$ when the $\mathbf{j}$ component is zero \\
$2t - 1 = 0$ \\
$2t = 1$ \\
$t = 0.5$ \\
(At $t = 0.5$ the $\mathbf{i}$ component is $-1 - 2 = -3$, which is not zero, so the acceleration is non-zero and parallel to $\mathbf{i}$)

@q 4
@topic Constant acceleration, speed, position
@marks 10
@question
(a) Use $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{a}t$ with $\mathbf{u} = -12\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}$ and $t = 10$ \\
$\mathbf{a}t = 10(1.6\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j}) = 16\mathbf{i} - 10\mathbf{j}$ \\
$\mathbf{v}_{B} = (-12\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j}) + (16\mathbf{i} - 10\mathbf{j})$ \\
$\mathbf{i}$ component: $-12 + 16 = 4$ \\
$\mathbf{j}$ component: $4 - 10 = -6$ \\
$\mathbf{v}_{B} = 4\mathbf{i} - 6\mathbf{j}$ \\
Speed $= \sqrt{4^{2} + (-6)^{2}}$ \\
$= \sqrt{16 + 36}$ \\
$= \sqrt{52}$ \\
Pull out the square factor: $\sqrt{52} = \sqrt{4 \times 13} = 2\sqrt{13}$ \\
Speed $= 7.21$ m s$^{-1}$ \\
(b) Use $\mathbf{r}_{C} = \mathbf{r}_{A} + \mathbf{u}T + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{a}T^{2}$ where $\mathbf{u}$ is the velocity at $A$ \\
$6\mathbf{i} + c\mathbf{j} = (34.8\mathbf{i} + 20\mathbf{j}) + (-12\mathbf{i} + 4\mathbf{j})T + \frac{1}{2}(1.6\mathbf{i} - \mathbf{j})T^{2}$ \\
$\mathbf{i}$ components: \\
$6 = 34.8 - 12T + 0.8T^{2}$ \\
Subtract 6 from both sides: \\
$0 = 28.8 - 12T + 0.8T^{2}$ \\
$0.8T^{2} - 12T + 28.8 = 0$ \\
Divide every term by 0.8: \\
$T^{2} - 15T + 36 = 0$ \\
Factorise: \\
$(T - 3)(T - 12) = 0$ \\
$T = 3$ or $T = 12$ \\
We are told $T > 10$, so reject $T = 3$ \\
$T = 12$ \\
(c) The $\mathbf{j}$ components, with $T = 12$: \\
$c = 20 + 4T - \frac{1}{2}T^{2}$ \\
$4T = 4 \times 12 = 48$ \\
$\frac{1}{2}T^{2} = \frac{1}{2} \times 144 = 72$ \\
$c = 20 + 48 - 72$ \\
$c = -4$

@q 5
@topic Projectile, tan equation, max height
@marks 11
@question
(a) Take $g = 9.8$ m s$^{-2}$ \\
Resolve the speed 35 m s$^{-1}$ at angle $\alpha$: horizontal $35\cos \alpha$, vertical $35\sin \alpha$ \\
Horizontal motion is at constant speed. $A$ is 60 m horizontally from $O$ after $T$ seconds: \\
$35\cos \alpha \times T = 60$ \\
Divide by $35\cos \alpha$: \\
$T = \frac{60}{35\cos \alpha}$ \\
Divide the top and bottom by 5: \\
$T = \frac{12}{7\cos \alpha}$ as required \\
(b) Vertical motion: $A$ is 45.6 m above $O$. Use $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}$ with $a = -g$: \\
$45.6 = 35\sin \alpha \times T - \frac{1}{2}gT^{2}$ \\
Put in $T = \frac{12}{7\cos \alpha}$: \\
$35\sin \alpha \times \frac{12}{7\cos \alpha} = \frac{35 \times 12}{7} \times \frac{\sin \alpha}{\cos \alpha}$ \\
$\frac{35 \times 12}{7} = 60$ \\
$= 60\tan \alpha$ \\
$\frac{1}{2}gT^{2} = \frac{1}{2} \times 9.8 \times \frac{144}{49\cos^{2} \alpha}$ \\
$4.9 \times \frac{144}{49} = 14.4$ \\
$= \frac{14.4}{\cos^{2} \alpha}$ \\
$\frac{1}{\cos^{2} \alpha} = \sec^{2} \alpha$, so $= 14.4\sec^{2} \alpha$ \\
$45.6 = 60\tan \alpha - 14.4\sec^{2} \alpha$ \\
Use $\sec^{2} \alpha = 1 + \tan^{2} \alpha$: \\
$45.6 = 60\tan \alpha - 14.4(1 + \tan^{2} \alpha)$ \\
Expand the bracket: \\
$45.6 = 60\tan \alpha - 14.4 - 14.4\tan^{2} \alpha$ \\
Add $14.4\tan^{2} \alpha$ and add 14.4 to both sides, subtract $60\tan \alpha$: \\
$14.4\tan^{2} \alpha - 60\tan \alpha + 14.4 + 45.6 = 0$ \\
$14.4 + 45.6 = 60$ \\
$14.4\tan^{2} \alpha - 60\tan \alpha + 60 = 0$ \\
Divide every term by 2.4: \\
$6\tan^{2} \alpha - 25\tan \alpha + 25 = 0$ as required \\
(c) Solve the quadratic: factorise \\
$6\tan^{2} \alpha - 25\tan \alpha + 25 = (2\tan \alpha - 5)(3\tan \alpha - 5)$ \\
Check: $2 \times 3 = 6$, $2 \times (-5) + (-5) \times 3 = -25$, $(-5)(-5) = 25$ \\
$\tan \alpha = \frac{5}{2}$ or $\tan \alpha = \frac{5}{3}$ \\
The greater the angle, the greater the height, so the greatest possible height comes from the larger angle, $\tan \alpha = 2.5$ \\
$\tan \alpha = 2.5$: a right angled triangle has opposite 2.5, adjacent 1, hypotenuse $\sqrt{7.25}$ \\
$\sin \alpha = \frac{2.5}{\sqrt{7.25}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{29}}$ \\
$\sin \alpha = 0.92848$ \\
Initial vertical speed: $35\sin \alpha = 35 \times 0.92848$ \\
$= 32.497$ m s$^{-1}$ \\
At the greatest height the vertical speed is 0 \\
Use $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as$: \\
$0 = 32.497^{2} - 2(9.8)H$ \\
$32.497^{2} = 1056.1$ \\
$0 = 1056.1 - 19.6H$ \\
Add $19.6H$: \\
$19.6H = 1056.1$ \\
$H = 53.88$ \\
The greatest possible height is 53.9 m \\
(With $\tan \alpha = \frac{5}{3}$ the height would be 46.0 m, which is smaller) \\
(d) Another limitation: the ball is modelled as a particle, but a real ball has a size and spin \\
(Or the wind could affect the motion)

@q 6
@topic Rod on rough wall, friction, new model
@marks 13
@question
(a) The rod leans against the wall on the right, so the wall pushes the rod to the left at $B$ with the normal reaction $S$ \\
The rod is in equilibrium, so the horizontal forces balance: the force at $A$ must push to the right \\
Friction at $A$ is the only horizontal force at $A$ \\
So the friction at $A$ acts to the right (towards the wall) \\
This also makes sense because the weight of the rod makes the end $A$ tend to slip away from the wall, and friction opposes this \\
(b) Take moments about $A$, with $B$ at horizontal distance $2a\cos \theta$ and height $2a\sin \theta$ \\
Moment of $S$ (horizontal at $B$): $S \times 2a\sin \theta$ \\
Moment of the weight $Mg$ at the midpoint (horizontal distance $a\cos \theta$): $Mg \times a\cos \theta$ \\
Moment of the friction $\frac{1}{7}Mg$ at $B$ (vertical, upwards, horizontal distance $2a\cos \theta$): $\frac{1}{7}Mg \times 2a\cos \theta$ \\
$S$ and the friction at $B$ turn the rod one way, the weight turns it the other way \\
$S \times 2a\sin \theta + \frac{1}{7}Mg \times 2a\cos \theta = Mga\cos \theta$ \\
Subtract $\frac{1}{7}Mg \times 2a\cos \theta$ from both sides: \\
$2aS\sin \theta = Mga\cos \theta - \frac{2}{7}Mga\cos \theta$ \\
Write $Mga\cos \theta$ as $\frac{7}{7}Mga\cos \theta$: \\
$2aS\sin \theta = \frac{5}{7}Mga\cos \theta$ \\
Divide both sides by $2a\sin \theta$: \\
$S = \frac{5}{14}Mg\frac{\cos \theta}{\sin \theta}$ \\
$S = \frac{5}{14}Mg\cot \theta$ as required \\
(c) $\tan \theta = \frac{5}{6}$, so $\cot \theta = \frac{6}{5}$ \\
$S = \frac{5}{14}Mg \times \frac{6}{5}$ \\
$S = \frac{6}{14}Mg$ \\
$S = \frac{3}{7}Mg$ \\
Resolve vertically: the ground reaction $R$ is upwards, the friction at $B$ is upwards, the weight is downwards: \\
$R + \frac{1}{7}Mg = Mg$ \\
$R = Mg - \frac{1}{7}Mg$ \\
$R = \frac{6}{7}Mg$ \\
Resolve horizontally: the friction $F$ at $A$ balances $S$: \\
$F = S = \frac{3}{7}Mg$ \\
The rod is in limiting equilibrium, so $F = \mu R$: \\
$\frac{3}{7}Mg = \mu \times \frac{6}{7}Mg$ \\
Divide both sides by $\frac{6}{7}Mg$: \\
$\mu = \frac{\frac{3}{7}}{\frac{6}{7}}$ \\
$\mu = \frac{3}{6}$ \\
$\mu = \frac{1}{2}$ \\
(d) The resultant force at $A$ is made of the horizontal friction $F$ and the vertical reaction $R$, at right angles to each other \\
$F = \frac{3}{7}Mg$ and $R = \frac{6}{7}Mg$ \\
Use Pythagoras: \\
$\text{Force} = \sqrt{F^{2} + R^{2}}$ \\
$F^{2} = \frac{9}{49}M^{2}g^{2}$ \\
$R^{2} = \frac{36}{49}M^{2}g^{2}$ \\
$\text{Force} = \sqrt{\frac{9 + 36}{49}}Mg$ \\
$= \sqrt{\frac{45}{49}}Mg$ \\
$\sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}$ and $\sqrt{49} = 7$ \\
$\text{Force} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{7}Mg$ \\
$\text{Force} = 0.958Mg$ \\
(e) In the new model the centre of mass is closer to $A$ \\
So the weight acts at a smaller horizontal distance from $A$, and its moment about $A$ is smaller \\
In the moments equation the weight's moment is balanced by $S$ and the friction at $B$ \\
A smaller moment from the weight needs a smaller $S$ \\
So the new value of $S$ is smaller
