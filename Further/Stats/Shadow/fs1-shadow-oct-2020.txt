@q 1
@topic Poisson test and Type II error
@marks 13
@question
The number of faulty lightbulbs produced by a machine follows a Poisson distribution. Past information shows that there will be a faulty bulb at an average rate of 3 every 20 bulbs. \\
Using this information, \\
(a)(i) find the probability that exactly 8 light bulbs will be faulty in a random selection of 100 bulbs. (2) \\
(ii) find the probability that at least 10 bulbs will be faulty in a random selection of 100 bulbs. (2) \\
Alterations are carried out on the machines that produce the bulbs. Following these alterations, the number of lightbulbs which are faulty over a randomly selected 60 bulbs is found to be 5. \\
(b) Test, at the 5% significance level, whether or not there is evidence of a decrease in the rate of faulty bulbs. State your hypotheses clearly. (4) \\
A further randomly selected 60 bulbs are tested and the hypothesis test is repeated. Given that the true rate of faulty bulbs is now 1 every 12 bulbs, \\
(c) calculate the probability of a Type II error. (5)
@scheme
(a)(i) For 100 bulbs, the Poisson mean is $15$. B1 \\
$P(X=8)=e^{-15}\frac{15^8}{8!}=0.019444\ldots$, about $0.0194$. B1 (2) \\
(ii) $P(X\geq10)=1-P(X\leq9)$. M1 \\
$=0.930146\ldots$, about $0.930$. A1 (2) \\
(b) $H_0$: rate $3$ per $20$ bulbs, so mean $9$ per $60$; $H_1$: rate is lower. B1 \\
Under $H_0$, $P(Y\leq5)=0.115690\ldots$. B1 \\
This is above $0.05$, so do not reject $H_0$. M1 \\
There is insufficient evidence that the faulty bulb rate has decreased. A1 (4) \\
(c) Under the null mean $9$, the 5% lower critical region is $Y\leq3$; do not reject for $Y\geq4$. M1, A1 \\
At the true rate, the mean for 60 bulbs is $60/12=5$. B1 \\
The Type II error probability is $P(Y\geq4)$ at Poisson mean $5$. M1 \\
$=1-P(Y\leq3)=0.734974\ldots$, about $0.735$. A1 (5)
@model
(a)(i) Scale the rate to 100 bulbs: $100\frac{3}{20}=15$. \\
Let $X$ count faulty bulbs among the 100; it has Poisson mean $15$. \\
Use $P(X=k)=e^{-15}\frac{15^k}{k!}$. \\
$P(X=8)=e^{-15}\frac{15^8}{8!}$ \\
$=0.0194443003\ldots\approx0.0194$. \\
(ii) Use the complement of $X\leq9$. \\
$P(X\geq10)=1-P(X\leq9)$ \\
$=1-0.0698536607\ldots$ \\
$=0.9301463393\ldots\approx0.930$. \\
(b) Let $Y$ count faulty bulbs among 60 after the changes. \\
Under $H_0$, the rate has not decreased: Poisson mean $60\frac{3}{20}=9$. \\
Under $H_1$, the faulty rate is lower than $3$ per $20$ bulbs. \\
For a lower-tail test, use $P(Y\leq5\text{ when mean}=9)$. \\
$=e^{-9}\sum_{k=0}^{5}\frac{9^k}{k!}$ \\
$=0.11569052\ldots$. \\
Since $0.11569052\ldots>0.05$, do not reject $H_0$. \\
There is insufficient evidence at 5% that the faulty rate has decreased. \\
(c) Under $H_0$, $P(Y\leq3)=0.021226\ldots<0.05$. \\
But $P(Y\leq4)=0.054963\ldots>0.05$. \\
Hence the critical region is $Y\leq3$. \\
A Type II error occurs when $Y\geq4$ despite a true decrease. \\
At the true rate of 1 faulty bulb per 12, the mean in 60 bulbs is $60/12=5$. \\
Let $W$ have a Poisson distribution with mean $5$. \\
$P(\text{Type II error})=P(W\geq4)$ \\
$=1-P(W\leq3)$ \\
$=1-0.2650259153\ldots$ \\
$=0.7349740847\ldots\approx0.735$.

@question
The discrete random variables $W$, $X$ and $Y$ are distributed as follows: $W$ is binomial with $n=10$, $p=0.3$; $X$ has a Poisson distribution with mean $3$; $Y$ has a Poisson distribution with mean $6$. \\
(a) Explain whether or not a Poisson distribution with mean $3$ would be a good approximation to a binomial distribution with $n=8$, $p=0.3$. (1) \\
(b) State the assumption required for $X+Y$ to be distributed as Poisson with mean $9$. (1) \\
Given that the assumption in part (b) holds, \\
(c) find $P(X+Y<\mathrm{Var}(W))$. (2)
@scheme
(a) A Poisson approximation requires large $n$ and small $p$; $n=8$ is small and $p=0.3$ is not small, so it is poor. B1 (1) \\
(b) $X$ and $Y$ must be independent. B1 (1) \\
(c) From the printed $W$ distribution, $\mathrm{Var}(W)=10(0.3)(0.7)=2.1$. M1 \\
With $X+Y$ having Poisson mean $9$, $P(X+Y<2.1)=P(X+Y\leq2)=e^{-9}(1+9+\frac{9^2}{2})=0.00623220\ldots$. A1 (2) \\
Note: The supplied shadow scheme uses $1.68$ from a binomial distribution with $n=8$ in part (c). The question defines $W$ with $n=10$; $2.1$ and $0.00623220\ldots$ follow from the printed question.
@model
(a) The approximation needs many trials and a small event probability. \\
Here $n=8$ is small and $p=0.3$ is not small, so Poisson mean $3$ is not a good approximation. \\
(b) $X$ and $Y$ must be independent for their Poisson means to add. \\
(c) For binomial $W$, use $\mathrm{Var}(W)=np(1-p)$. \\
$=10(0.3)(0.7)$ \\
$=2.1$. \\
By independence, $X+Y$ has Poisson mean $3+6=9$. \\
Since the sum is integer valued, $X+Y<2.1$ means $X+Y\leq2$. \\
$P(X+Y\leq2)=P(0)+P(1)+P(2)$ \\
$=e^{-9}\left(1+9+\frac{9^2}{2!}\right)$ \\
$=0.0062321951\ldots\approx0.00623$.

@q 3
@topic Geometric game and infinite series
@marks 9
@question
Beth and Amal are playing a game. They roll a fair dice. If Beth rolls a 2 she wins, otherwise Amal rolls the dice. If he rolls a 2 he wins the game. Otherwise they repeat this process until one of them rolls a 2. \\
(a) Find the probability that Beth rolls a 2 on her third go. (2) \\
(b) Find the probability that a 2 is rolled on or after Amal's fourth go. (2) \\
(c) Find the mean and standard deviation of the number of rolls before a 2 is seen. (2) \\
(d) Find the probability that Beth wins the game. (3)
@scheme
(a) Beth's third roll is the fifth roll overall, so $P=\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4\frac{1}{6}=0.0803755\ldots$, about $0.0804$. M1, A1 (2) \\
(b) A 2 on or after Amal's fourth go requires no 2 in the first seven rolls: $P=\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^7=0.2790816\ldots$. M1, A1 (2) \\
(c) Geometric distribution with $p=\frac{1}{6}$ gives mean $1/p=6$. B1 \\
Standard deviation $=\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p^2}}=\sqrt{30}=5.477\ldots$, about $5.48$. B1 (2) \\
(d) Beth wins on rolls $1,3,5,\ldots$, so $P=\frac{1}{6}+\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^2\frac{1}{6}+\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4\frac{1}{6}+\cdots$. M1 \\
Sum the infinite geometric series: $P=\frac{1/6}{1-(5/6)^2}$. M1 \\
$=\frac{6}{11}$. A1 (3)
@model
(a) Beth's third go is roll 5 overall. \\
For her to win then, the first four rolls are not 2 and roll 5 is 2. \\
$P=\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)$ \\
$=\frac{625}{7776}$ \\
$=0.0803755144\ldots\approx0.0804$. \\
(b) Amal's fourth go is roll 8 overall. \\
For the first 2 to occur on roll 8 or later, rolls 1 to 7 must all miss 2. \\
$P=\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^7$ \\
$=0.2790816472\ldots\approx0.279$. \\
(c) Let $T$ be the number of rolls up to and including the first 2. \\
$T$ has a geometric distribution with $p=\frac{1}{6}$. \\
$E(T)=\frac{1}{p}=6$. \\
$\mathrm{Var}(T)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}$ \\
$=\frac{5/6}{(1/6)^2}=30$. \\
The standard deviation is $\sqrt{30}=5.477225\ldots\approx5.48$. \\
(d) Beth wins if she succeeds on roll 1, 3, 5, and so on. \\
Each further chance requires two unsuccessful rolls. \\
$P(\text{Beth wins})=\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^2+\frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4+\cdots$. \\
This is a geometric series with first term $\frac{1}{6}$ and ratio $\frac{25}{36}$. \\
Its sum is $\frac{1/6}{1-25/36}$. \\
$=\frac{1/6}{11/36}$ \\
$=\frac{6}{11}\approx0.545$.

@topic Discrete transformation and moments
@marks 8
@question
The discrete random variable $X$ has the following probability distribution. \\
$x$: $-3,-1,3,9$; $P(X=x)$: $\frac{2}{5},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{15},\frac{1}{5}$. \\
(a) Find $\mathrm{Var}(X)$. (3) \\
The discrete random variable $Y$ is defined in terms of $X$. When $X$ is negative, $Y=2X^2$. When $X$ is positive, $Y=X+4$. \\
(b) Find $P(Y\geq8)$. (3) \\
(c) Find $E(XY)$. (2)
@scheme
(a) $E(X)=(-3)\frac{2}{5}+(-1)\frac{1}{3}+3\frac{1}{15}+9\frac{1}{5}=\frac{7}{15}$. M1 \\
$E(X^2)=9\frac{2}{5}+1\frac{1}{3}+9\frac{1}{15}+81\frac{1}{5}=\frac{311}{15}$. M1 \\
$\mathrm{Var}(X)=\frac{311}{15}-\left(\frac{7}{15}\right)^2=\frac{4616}{225}=20.5156\ldots$. A1 (3) \\
(b) For $x=-3,-1,3,9$, the corresponding $y$ values are $18,2,7,13$. M1 \\
$P(Y\geq8)=P(X=-3)+P(X=9)$. M1 \\
$=\frac{2}{5}+\frac{1}{5}=\frac{3}{5}$. A1 (3) \\
(c) $E(XY)=(-3)(18)\frac{2}{5}+(-1)(2)\frac{1}{3}+3(7)\frac{1}{15}+9(13)\frac{1}{5}$. M1 \\
$=\frac{38}{15}$. A1 (2) \\
Note: The supplied shadow scheme writes $P(X=4)$ in part (b); the printed table has $X=9$. The event and final $\frac{3}{5}$ are preserved here.
@model
(a) Use $E(X)=\sum xP(X=x)$. \\
$E(X)=(-3)\frac{2}{5}+(-1)\frac{1}{3}+3\frac{1}{15}+9\frac{1}{5}$ \\
$=-\frac{6}{5}-\frac{1}{3}+\frac{1}{5}+\frac{9}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{7}{15}$. \\
Use $E(X^2)=\sum x^2P(X=x)$. \\
$E(X^2)=9\frac{2}{5}+1\frac{1}{3}+9\frac{1}{15}+81\frac{1}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{18}{5}+\frac{1}{3}+\frac{3}{5}+\frac{81}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{311}{15}$. \\
$\mathrm{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2$ \\
$=\frac{311}{15}-\left(\frac{7}{15}\right)^2$ \\
$=\frac{4665-49}{225}$ \\
$=\frac{4616}{225}=20.5155\ldots$. \\
(b) Map each possible $X$ to $Y$. \\
For $X=-3$, $Y=2(-3)^2=18$. \\
For $X=-1$, $Y=2(-1)^2=2$. \\
For $X=3$, $Y=3+4=7$. \\
For $X=9$, $Y=9+4=13$. \\
Thus $Y\geq8$ when $X=-3$ or $X=9$. \\
$P(Y\geq8)=\frac{2}{5}+\frac{1}{5}=\frac{3}{5}$. \\
(c) Use $E(XY)=\sum xyP(X=x)$. \\
$=(-3)(18)\frac{2}{5}+(-1)(2)\frac{1}{3}+3(7)\frac{1}{15}+9(13)\frac{1}{5}$ \\
$=-\frac{108}{5}-\frac{2}{3}+\frac{7}{5}+\frac{117}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{16}{5}-\frac{2}{3}$ \\
$=\frac{48-10}{15}$ \\
$=\frac{38}{15}$.

@q 5
@topic Binomial goodness of fit
@marks 14
@question
A total of 120 random samples of 7 items are selected from a production line in a factory and the number of defective items in each sample is recorded. The results are summarised in the table below. \\
Number of defective items: $0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7$. Observed number of samples: $2,6,11,19,25,32,16,9$. \\
(a) Write down two reasons why a binomial distribution may be a suitable model. (2) \\
(b) Show that the probability of a randomly selected item from this sample being faulty is $0.6$. (2) \\
The research station used a binomial distribution with probability $0.6$ of a faulty item. The expected frequencies were calculated to 2 decimal places. The results are as follows. \\
Number of defective items: $0,1,2,3,4,5,6,7$. Expected number of samples: $0.20,2.06,s,23.22,t,31.35,15.68,3.36$. \\
(c) Find the value of $s$ and the value of $t$. (2) \\
(d) Stating your hypotheses clearly, test at the 1% level of significance whether or not the data can be modelled by a binomial distribution. State your hypothesis clearly. (8)
@scheme
(a) Items are independent; each sample has a fixed number of items; each item is faulty or not; the faulty probability is constant. Any two. B1, B1 (2) \\
(b) There are $0(2)+1(6)+2(11)+3(19)+4(25)+5(32)+6(16)+7(9)=504$ faulty items among $120(7)=840$ items. M1 \\
$504/840=0.6$. A1 (2) \\
(c) $s=120(7C2)(0.4)^5(0.6)^2=9.29$. B1 \\
$t=120(7C4)(0.4)^3(0.6)^4=34.84$. B1 (2) \\
(d) $H_0$: binomial with $n=7$, $p=0.6$ is suitable; $H_1$: it is not. B1 \\
Combine groups $0,1,2$ and groups $6,7$. Observed frequencies are $19,19,25,32,25$; expected are $11.55,23.22,34.84,31.35,19.04$. M2 \\
With five groups and one estimated parameter, degrees of freedom $=5-2=3$. B1ft \\
The 1% upper critical value is $11.345$. B1ft \\
Test statistic $=\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}=10.2306\ldots$. M1, A1 \\
$10.2306\ldots<11.345$, so do not reject $H_0$; the binomial model is suitable at the 1% level. A1 (8)
@model
(a) Each sample contains a fixed number, seven, of items. \\
Each item has just two outcomes, faulty or not faulty, with a constant probability. \\
The binomial model also assumes independence between items. \\
(b) Count faulty items across the 120 samples: \\
$0(2)+1(6)+2(11)+3(19)+4(25)+5(32)+6(16)+7(9)$ \\
$=0+6+22+57+100+160+96+63$ \\
$=504$. \\
There are $120(7)=840$ inspected items. \\
The observed faulty proportion is $\frac{504}{840}=\frac{3}{5}=0.6$, as required. \\
(c) For $Z$ binomial with $n=7$, $p=0.6$, use $P(Z=k)=7Ck(0.6)^k(0.4)^{7-k}$. \\
$s=120P(Z=2)$ \\
$=120(7C2)(0.6)^2(0.4)^5$ \\
$=9.289728\ldots=9.29$. \\
$t=120P(Z=4)$ \\
$=120(7C4)(0.6)^4(0.4)^3$ \\
$=34.83648\ldots=34.84$. \\
(d) Under $H_0$, the binomial distribution with $n=7$, $p=0.6$ is suitable. \\
Under $H_1$, it is not suitable. \\
Combine groups with expected counts under five. \\
For $0$ to $2$, $O=2+6+11=19$ and $E=0.20+2.06+9.29=11.55$. \\
For $6$ to $7$, $O=16+9=25$ and $E=15.68+3.36=19.04$. \\
The five combined $(O,E)$ pairs are $(19,11.55)$, $(19,23.22)$, $(25,34.84)$, $(32,31.35)$, $(25,19.04)$. \\
Since $p$ was estimated from the data, degrees of freedom $=5-1-1=3$. \\
The 1% upper critical value is $11.345$. \\
Compute $\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$: \\
$\frac{(19-11.55)^2}{11.55}+\frac{(19-23.22)^2}{23.22}+\frac{(25-34.84)^2}{34.84}$ \\
$+\frac{(32-31.35)^2}{31.35}+\frac{(25-19.04)^2}{19.04}$ \\
$=10.230611\ldots$. \\
Since $10.230611\ldots<11.345$, do not reject $H_0$. \\
At 1%, the data are consistent with the proposed binomial model.

@q 6
@topic Probability generating function moments
@marks 12
@question
A discrete random variable $X$ has probability generating function given by $G_X(t)=a+at+bt^6$, where $a$ and $b$ are positive constants. \\
(a) Write down the value of $P(X=3)$. (1) \\
Given that $E(X)=4$, \\
(b)(i) find $a$ and $b$. (6) \\
(ii) find $\mathrm{Var}(X)$. (5)
@scheme
(a) There is no $t^3$ term, so $P(X=3)=0$. B1 (1) \\
(b)(i) $G_X(1)=1$ gives $2a+b=1$. B1 \\
$G'_X(t)=a+6bt^5$, and $E(X)=G'_X(1)=a+6b=4$. M1, A1 \\
Solve $2a+b=1$ and $a+6b=4$. M1 \\
$a=\frac{2}{11}$ and $b=\frac{7}{11}$. A2 (6) \\
(ii) $G'_X(t)=a+6bt^5$. M1 \\
$G''_X(t)=30bt^4$. M1 \\
$G''_X(1)=30b=\frac{210}{11}$. A1 \\
$\mathrm{Var}(X)=G''_X(1)+E(X)-[E(X)]^2=\frac{210}{11}+4-16$. M1 \\
$=\frac{78}{11}$. A1 (5)
@model
(a) The coefficient of $t^3$ in $G_X(t)$ is zero. \\
Therefore $P(X=3)=0$. \\
(b)(i) A generating function satisfies $G_X(1)=1$. \\
Substitute $t=1$: $a+a+b=1$. \\
Thus $2a+b=1$. \\
Differentiate: $G'_X(t)=a+6bt^5$. \\
Use $E(X)=G'_X(1)=4$: $a+6b=4$. \\
Multiply the second equation by $2$: $2a+12b=8$. \\
Subtract $2a+b=1$: $11b=7$. \\
$b=\frac{7}{11}$. \\
Substitute into $2a+b=1$: $2a+\frac{7}{11}=1$. \\
$2a=\frac{4}{11}$. \\
$a=\frac{2}{11}$. \\
(ii) Differentiate $G'_X(t)=a+6bt^5$ again: \\
$G''_X(t)=30bt^4$. \\
At $t=1$, $G''_X(1)=30b=30\frac{7}{11}=\frac{210}{11}$. \\
Use $G''_X(1)=E[X(X-1)]$. \\
Hence $\mathrm{Var}(X)=G''_X(1)+E(X)-[E(X)]^2$. \\
$=\frac{210}{11}+4-4^2$ \\
$=\frac{210}{11}-12$ \\
$=\frac{210-132}{11}$ \\
$=\frac{78}{11}=7.090909\ldots$.

@q 7
@topic Central limit theorem and test power
@marks 15
@question
A five-sided spinner has sides labelled 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. The random variable $S$ represents the score when the spinner is spun. Helen rolls the spinner 50 times and the mean score is calculated. \\
Assuming the spinner is fair and using a suitable approximation, \\
(a) find, to 3 significant figures, the value of $k$ such that $P(\text{mean score}<k)=0.05$. (8) \\
(b) Explain the relevance of the Central Limit Theorem in part (a). (2) \\
Helen considers the following hypotheses: $H_0$: the spinner is fair; $H_1$: the spinner is not fair. If the mean score is below $2.5$ or above $3.1$, then $H_0$ will be rejected. \\
Given that the true distribution of $S$ has mean $3.2$ and variance $2.5$, \\
(c) find the power of this test. (3) \\
(d) Describe what would happen to the power of this test if Helen were to increase the number of spins. Give a reason for your answer. (2)
@scheme
(a) For a fair spinner, $S$ is uniform on $1,2,3,4,5$. M1 \\
$E(S)=\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{5}=3$. M1 \\
$E(S^2)=\frac{1+4+9+16+25}{5}=11$; $\mathrm{Var}(S)=11-3^2=2$. M1, A1 \\
For 50 spins, the sample mean is approximately normal with mean $3$ and variance $2/50=0.04$. M1, A1 \\
$P(\text{mean score}<k)=0.05$ gives $\frac{k-3}{\sqrt{0.04}}=-1.64485\ldots$. M1 \\
$k=2.671029\ldots$, about $2.67$ to 3 significant figures. A1 (8) \\
(b) The sample size $50$ is large enough for the Central Limit Theorem. B1 \\
It gives an approximately normal distribution for the sample mean even though $S$ is discrete uniform. B1 (2) \\
(c) At the true distribution, the sample mean is approximately normal with mean $3.2$ and variance $2.5/50=0.05$. M1 \\
Power $=P(\text{mean score}<2.5)+P(\text{mean score}>3.1)$. M1 \\
$=0.000872559\ldots+0.672639577\ldots=0.673512136\ldots$, about $0.674$. A1 (3) \\
(d) Increasing the number of spins decreases the variance of the sample mean. B1 \\
The true mean $3.2$ is above $3.1$, so the probability of rejection, hence the power, increases. dB1 (2) \\
Note: The supplied shadow scheme reports $0.3265$ for part (c), the probability of acceptance. The printed rejection rule gives power $1-0.326487864\ldots=0.673512136\ldots$.
@model
(a) For a fair spinner, each of $1,2,3,4,5$ has probability $\frac{1}{5}$. \\
Use $E(S)=\sum sP(S=s)$. \\
$E(S)=\frac{1+2+3+4+5}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{15}{5}=3$. \\
Use $E(S^2)=\sum s^2P(S=s)$. \\
$E(S^2)=\frac{1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2+5^2}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{1+4+9+16+25}{5}$ \\
$=\frac{55}{5}=11$. \\
$\mathrm{Var}(S)=E(S^2)-[E(S)]^2$ \\
$=11-3^2=2$. \\
For 50 independent spins, the central limit theorem gives an approximately normal sample mean. \\
Its mean is $3$ and its variance is $\frac{2}{50}=0.04$. \\
Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.04}=0.2$. \\
The 5% lower standard normal quantile is $z=-1.6448536\ldots$. \\
Standardise $k$: $\frac{k-3}{0.2}=-1.6448536\ldots$. \\
Multiply by $0.2$: $k-3=-0.3289707\ldots$. \\
$k=2.6710293\ldots$. \\
So $k=2.67$ to 3 significant figures. \\
(b) The individual score $S$ is discrete uniform, not normal. \\
With 50 independent spins, the Central Limit Theorem makes the sample mean approximately normal, so the normal quantile in part (a) can be used. \\
(c) Under the true distribution, $E(S)=3.2$ and $\mathrm{Var}(S)=2.5$. \\
For 50 spins, the sample mean has approximate mean $3.2$ and variance $\frac{2.5}{50}=0.05$. \\
Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.05}=0.2236068\ldots$. \\
The test rejects when the sample mean is below $2.5$ or above $3.1$. \\
For the lower region, $z=\frac{2.5-3.2}{\sqrt{0.05}}=-3.130495\ldots$. \\
Thus $P(\text{mean score}<2.5)=0.000872559\ldots$. \\
For the upper region, $z=\frac{3.1-3.2}{\sqrt{0.05}}=-0.4472136\ldots$. \\
Thus $P(\text{mean score}>3.1)=0.672639577\ldots$. \\
Add the disjoint rejection probabilities: \\
Power $=0.000872559\ldots+0.672639577\ldots$ \\
$=0.673512136\ldots\approx0.674$. \\
(d) If Helen increases the number of spins to $n$, the sample mean's variance becomes $2.5/n$. \\
As $n$ increases, the sample mean concentrates around the true mean $3.2$. \\
Since $3.2>3.1$, rejection becomes more likely and the power increases.
