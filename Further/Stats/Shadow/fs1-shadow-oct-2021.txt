@q 1
@topic Chi-square goodness of fit
@marks 7
@question
Kelly throws a tetrahedral die $n$ times and records the number on which it lands for each throw. She calculates the expected frequency for each number to be 43 if the die was unbiased. The table below shows three of the frequencies Kelly records but the fourth one is missing. \\
Number: $1,2,3,4$. Frequency: $46,33,38,x$. \\
(a) Show that $x=55$. (1) \\
Kelly wishes to test, at the 5% level of significance, whether or not there is evidence that the tetrahedral die is unbiased. \\
(b) Explain why there are 3 degrees of freedom for this test. (1) \\
(c) Stating your hypotheses clearly and the critical value used, carry out the test. (5)
@scheme
(a) Total rolls $=4(43)=172$, so $x=172-46-33-38=55$. B1 3.4 (1) \\
(b) Four categories with only the total constrained give degrees of freedom $4-1=3$. B1 2.4 (1) \\
(c) $H_0$: die is unbiased; $H_1$: die is biased. B1 2.1 \\
Statistic $=\frac{(46-43)^2}{43}+\frac{(33-43)^2}{43}+\frac{(38-43)^2}{43}+\frac{(55-43)^2}{43}$. M1 1.1b \\
$=6.465116\ldots$. A1 1.1b \\
The 5% upper critical value with 3 degrees of freedom is $7.815$. B1 1.1b \\
Since $6.465116\ldots<7.815$, do not reject $H_0$; results are consistent with an unbiased die. A1 3.5a (5)
@model
(a) The expected total for four equal categories is $4(43)=172$ rolls. \\
The three known frequencies add to $46+33+38=117$. \\
Therefore $x=172-117=55$, as required. \\
(b) There are four categories. Their frequencies must sum to the fixed total, leaving $4-1=3$ independent frequencies. \\
No probability parameter is estimated, so degrees of freedom $=3$. \\
(c) Under $H_0$, the tetrahedral die is unbiased. \\
Under $H_1$, it is biased. \\
Each expected frequency is $43$. \\
Use $\sum\frac{(O-E)^2}{E}$. \\
$=\frac{(46-43)^2}{43}+\frac{(33-43)^2}{43}$ \\
$+\frac{(38-43)^2}{43}+\frac{(55-43)^2}{43}$ \\
$=\frac{9+100+25+144}{43}$ \\
$=\frac{278}{43}=6.465116\ldots$. \\
At 5% with three degrees of freedom, the critical value is $7.815$. \\
Since $6.465116\ldots<7.815$, do not reject $H_0$. \\
There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level that the die is biased.

@q 2
@topic Poisson process and approximation
@marks 14
@question
On a weekday, a factory monitors the randomly produced faulty items, at a mean rate of 1.2 per 15 minutes. \\
(a) Show that the probability that on a weekday at least 3 faults produced by the machine in a 60 minute slot is 0.857 to 3 decimal places. (2) \\
(b) Calculate the probability that at least 3 faults are recorded in fewer than 4 out of 7 randomly selected, non-overlapping 60-minute periods on a weekday. (2) \\
The manager of the factory randomly selects 120 non-overlapping 60-minute periods on weekdays. She records the number of faults produced in each of these 60-minute periods. \\
(c) Using a Poisson approximation show that the probability of the manager finding at least 4 of these 60-minute periods when exactly 10 faults are found is 0.103 to 3 significant figures. (4) \\
(d) Explain why the Poisson approximation may be reasonable in this case. (1) \\
The manager of the factory decided to investigate if the number of faults produced is different on a Saturday to a weekday. She selects a Saturday at random and records the number of faults produced in the first 3.5 hours. \\
(e) Write down the hypotheses for this test. (1) \\
The manager found that there had been 23 faults in the first 3.5 hours. \\
(f) Carry out the test using a 5% level of significance. (4)
@scheme
(a) For 60 minutes, the Poisson mean is $4(1.2)=4.8$. M1 3.3 \\
$P(F\geq3)=1-P(F\leq2)=0.857460\ldots$, so $0.857$ to 3 decimal places. A1 1.1b (2) \\
(b) Let $A$ count periods with at least 3 faults; $A$ is binomial with $n=7$, $p=0.857460\ldots$. M1 3.3 \\
$P(A<4)=P(A\leq3)=0.0100686\ldots$. A1 1.1b (2) \\
(c) $P(F=10)$ at Poisson mean $4.8$ is $0.0147243\ldots$. B1 1.1b \\
For 120 periods, count $E$ of periods with exactly 10 faults; Poisson approximation mean $=120(0.0147243\ldots)=1.76692\ldots$. M1 3.3 \\
$P(E\geq4)=1-P(E\leq3)$ with that mean. M1 3.4 \\
$=0.1034526\ldots=0.103$ to 3 significant figures. A1 2.1 (4) \\
(d) There are many periods and the probability of exactly 10 faults in one is small. B1 2.4 (1) \\
(e) $H_0$: Saturday mean for 3.5 hours is $16.8$; $H_1$: it is different from $16.8$. B1 2.5 (1) \\
(f) Under $H_0$, $X$ has Poisson mean $16.8$. B1 3.3 \\
$P(X\geq23)=1-P(X\leq22)$. M1 1.1b \\
$=0.0868735\ldots$. A1 1.1b \\
This exceeds the upper-tail allocation $0.025$ for a two-sided 5% test, so do not reject $H_0$; insufficient evidence of a Saturday difference. A1 2.2b (4)
@model
(a) A 60-minute period contains four 15-minute periods. \\
Its Poisson mean is $4(1.2)=4.8$. \\
Let $F$ count faults in 60 minutes. \\
$P(F\geq3)=1-P(F\leq2)$ \\
$=1-e^{-4.8}\left(1+4.8+\frac{4.8^2}{2!}\right)$ \\
$=0.8574607811\ldots$ \\
$=0.857$ to 3 decimal places, as required. \\
(b) Let $A$ count the selected periods with at least three faults. \\
Each independent period has success probability $p=0.8574607811\ldots$. \\
Thus $A$ has a binomial distribution with $n=7$ and this $p$. \\
"Fewer than four" means $A\leq3$. \\
$P(A\leq3)=\sum_{k=0}^{3}7Ck\,p^k(1-p)^{7-k}$ \\
$=0.0100685561\ldots\approx0.0101$. \\
(c) For one period, $q=P(F=10)=e^{-4.8}\frac{4.8^{10}}{10!}$. \\
$=0.0147243397\ldots$. \\
Across 120 periods, the binomial count of exact-tens has mean $120q$. \\
$=120(0.0147243397\ldots)=1.76692076\ldots$. \\
Approximate that count by a Poisson variable $E$ with this mean. \\
$P(E\geq4)=1-P(E\leq3)$ \\
$=1-e^{-1.76692076\ldots}\sum_{k=0}^{3}\frac{(1.76692076\ldots)^k}{k!}$ \\
$=0.1034526025\ldots$ \\
$=0.103$ to 3 significant figures, as required. \\
(d) The event of exactly ten faults has probability about $0.0147$ in each period, which is small, and there are 120 periods. \\
(e) Under $H_0$, the Saturday fault rate equals the weekday rate. \\
The 3.5-hour mean is $\frac{210}{15}(1.2)=16.8$. \\
Under $H_1$, the Saturday rate, hence the 3.5-hour mean, differs from this. \\
(f) Let $X$ count faults in the sampled 3.5 hours. \\
Under $H_0$, $X$ has a Poisson distribution with mean $16.8$. \\
Since the observed count $23$ is high, calculate the upper-tail probability. \\
$P(X\geq23)=1-P(X\leq22)$ \\
$=0.0868734961\ldots$. \\
The 5% two-sided test allocates $0.025$ to the upper tail. \\
Since $0.0868734961\ldots>0.025$, do not reject $H_0$. \\
There is insufficient evidence at 5% that Saturday's fault rate differs from the weekday rate.

@topic Binomial sample mean normal approximation
@marks 4
@question
A food delivery company delivers takeaway food. A random variable $X$ represents the number of food deliveries made each day by the company where $X$ has a binomial distribution with $n=300$, $p=0.72$. A random sample $X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_{100}$ is taken. \\
Estimate the probability that the mean number of food deliveries delivered each day by the company is greater than 217. (4)
@scheme
Use the central limit theorem for the sample mean. M1 3.1a \\
$E(X)=300(0.72)=216$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\text{sample mean})=\frac{300(0.72)(0.28)}{100}=0.6048$. A1 1.1b \\
$P(\text{sample mean}>217)\approx P\left(Z>\frac{217-216}{\sqrt{0.6048}}\right)$. dM1 3.4 \\
$=0.0992457\ldots$, about $0.0992$. A1 1.1b (4) \\
Note: The supplied scheme prints $\geq218$ in an intermediate line, while the question asks for greater than $217$. The displayed standardisation uses $217$.
@model
For one day, $E(X)=np=300(0.72)=216$. \\
Its variance is $np(1-p)=300(0.72)(0.28)$. \\
$=60.48$. \\
For 100 independent days, use the central limit theorem. \\
The sample mean is approximately normal with mean $216$. \\
Its variance is $\frac{60.48}{100}=0.6048$. \\
Its standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.6048}=0.777689\ldots$. \\
Standardise $217$: $z=\frac{217-216}{\sqrt{0.6048}}=1.28586\ldots$. \\
$P(\text{sample mean}>217)\approx P(Z>1.28586\ldots)$ \\
$=0.0992457448\ldots\approx0.0992$.

@q 4
@topic Conditional probability and moments
@marks 10
@question
A spinner with numbers 0 to 5 is used to play at a games club and has the following probability distribution. \\
$n$: $0,1,2,3,4,5$; $P(N=n)$: $0.1,a,0.25,b,c,0.2$. \\
Given that $E(2N+5)=9.6$ and $P(N=1\text{ given }N<3)=\frac{6}{13}$. The probability of landing on a 3 is twice the probability of landing on a 4. \\
(a) show that $\mathrm{Var}(N)=2.71$. (6) \\
The games club pay 20p if you land on 3, 4 or 5 otherwise you win 10p. \\
(b) calculate the expected winnings per player. (3) \\
Bai suggests that, as the mean and variance are close, a Poisson distribution could be used to model the amount won by a player. \\
(c) State a limitation of the Poisson distribution in this case. (1)
@scheme
(a) $E(2N+5)=2E(N)+5=9.6$, so $E(N)=2.3$. M1 3.1a \\
Thus $a+2(0.25)+3b+4c+5(0.2)=2.3$. M1 1.1b \\
$\frac{a}{0.1+a+0.25}=\frac{6}{13}$ gives $a=0.3$. M1 3.1a \\
Since $b=2c$ and total probability is $1$, $b=0.1$ and $c=0.05$. \\
$E(N^2)=1^2(0.3)+2^2(0.25)+3^2(0.1)+4^2(0.05)+5^2(0.2)=8$. M1 1.1b \\
$\mathrm{Var}(N)=8-(2.3)^2=2.71$. dM1 1.1b, A1* 2.1 (6) \\
(b) Winnings are 10p for $N=0,1,2$ and 20p for $N=3,4,5$. M1 3.3 \\
Expected winnings $=0.10(0.1+0.3+0.25)+0.20(0.1+0.05+0.2)$. M1 1.1b \\
$=0.135$ pounds, or 13.5p. A1 1.1b (3) \\
(c) A Poisson distribution assigns positive probability to amounts other than 10p and 20p, whereas these are the only possible winnings. B1 3.5b (1)
@model
(a) Use linearity of expectation: $E(2N+5)=2E(N)+5$. \\
$9.6=2E(N)+5$. \\
Subtract 5: $4.6=2E(N)$. \\
Divide by 2: $E(N)=2.3$. \\
The conditional probability is $\frac{P(N=1)}{P(N<3)}=\frac{6}{13}$. \\
$\frac{a}{0.1+a+0.25}=\frac{6}{13}$. \\
Combine constants: $\frac{a}{a+0.35}=\frac{6}{13}$. \\
Cross multiply: $13a=6a+2.1$. \\
Subtract $6a$: $7a=2.1$. \\
$a=0.3$. \\
The conditions give $b=2c$. \\
Probabilities sum to one: $0.1+0.3+0.25+b+c+0.2=1$. \\
Thus $b+c=0.15$. \\
Substitute $b=2c$: $3c=0.15$. \\
$c=0.05$ and $b=0.1$. \\
Compute the first moment to check: \\
$E(N)=1(0.3)+2(0.25)+3(0.1)+4(0.05)+5(0.2)=2.3$. \\
Compute $E(N^2)$: \\
$E(N^2)=1^2(0.3)+2^2(0.25)+3^2(0.1)+4^2(0.05)+5^2(0.2)$ \\
$=0.3+1+0.9+0.8+5$ \\
$=8$. \\
Use $\mathrm{Var}(N)=E(N^2)-[E(N)]^2$. \\
$=8-(2.3)^2$ \\
$=8-5.29$ \\
$=2.71$, as required. \\
(b) The chance of winning 10p is $0.1+0.3+0.25=0.65$. \\
The chance of winning 20p is $0.1+0.05+0.2=0.35$. \\
Expected winnings in pounds $=0.10(0.65)+0.20(0.35)$. \\
$=0.065+0.070$ \\
$=0.135$ pounds, or 13.5p per player. \\
(c) A Poisson variable can take many nonnegative integer values, while winnings here can only be 10p or 20p.

@q 5
@topic Geometric and negative binomial tests
@marks 18
@question
Three bags labelled A, B and C respectively contain a large number of marbles coloured either yellow or green. A marble is drawn from a bag one at a time, the colour is noted and returned to the bag. The probability of drawing a yellow marble in one go from bag A is 0.04. \\
(a) Find the probability of drawing at least 3 green marbles before getting a red from bag A. (2) \\
(b) Find the probability of drawing a second yellow marble from bag A on the 10th draw. (2) \\
The probability of getting a yellow marble on any one draw from bag B is $p$. A marble is drawn and replaced from bag B until $n$ yellow marbles are drawn. The random variable $D$ is the number of marbles drawn. \\
Given that the mean and the standard deviation of $D$ are 22500 and 300 respectively, \\
(c) find the value of $p$. (4) \\
Tom believes bag C has a smaller proportion of yellow marbles than bag A. To test this Tom draws marbles from bag C until he gets a yellow. Tom defines the random variable $J$ to be the number of marbles drawn up to and including the first yellow counter. \\
(d) Stating your hypotheses clearly and using a 5% level of significance, find the critical region for this test. (5) \\
Tom gets a yellow marble for the first time on his 24th draw. \\
(e) Giving a reason for your answer, state whether or not there is evidence that bag C contains a smaller proportion of yellow marbles than bag A. (2) \\
Given that the probability of getting a yellow from bag C on any one draw is 0.013, \\
(f) show that the power of the test is 0.380 to 3 significant figures. (3)
@scheme
(a) Interpreting "red" as the first yellow marble, the first three draws must be green: $(1-0.04)^3=0.884736$, about $0.885$. M1 1.1b, A1 1.1b (2) \\
(b) Exactly one yellow in the first nine draws, followed by yellow on draw 10: $9(0.96)^8(0.04)^2=0.0103880\ldots$, about $0.0104$. M1 3.3, A1 1.1b (2) \\
(c) Negative binomial mean $\frac{n}{p}=22500$ and variance $\frac{n(1-p)}{p^2}=300^2$. M1 3.1b, A1 1.1b \\
Divide variance by mean: $\frac{1-p}{p}=\frac{90000}{22500}=4$, so $1-p=4p$. M1 1.1b \\
$p=0.2$. A1 1.1b (4) \\
(d) $H_0:p=0.04$; $H_1:p<0.04$. B1 2.5 \\
Under $H_0$, $J$ is geometric with $p=0.04$. M1 3.3 \\
For critical region $J\geq c$, $P(J\geq c)=(0.96)^{c-1}<0.05$. M1 3.4 \\
$c-1>\frac{\log(0.05)}{\log(0.96)}=73.38525\ldots$. M1 1.1b \\
The smallest integer is $c=75$; critical region $J\geq75$. A1 1.1b (5) \\
(e) $24$ is not in the critical region $J\geq75$. M1 1.1b \\
There is insufficient evidence at 5% that bag C has a smaller yellow proportion than bag A. A1 2.2b (2) \\
(f) At true $p=0.013$, power $=P(J\geq75)$. M1 2.1 \\
$=(1-0.013)^{74}$. M1 1.1b \\
$=0.379725\ldots=0.380$ to 3 significant figures. A1* 1.1b (3) \\
Note: The question says "red" in part (a), although the bags contain only yellow or green marbles. The supplied scheme evaluates waiting for the first yellow marble.
@model
(a) The source says "red", but defines only yellow and green marbles. The intended event in the scheme is three green draws before the first yellow. \\
Each green draw has probability $1-0.04=0.96$. \\
The first three must all be green: \\
$P=(0.96)^3$ \\
$=0.884736\approx0.885$. \\
(b) For the second yellow to be on draw 10, exactly one of the first nine draws must be yellow and draw 10 yellow. \\
Choose the first yellow's place in $9$ ways. \\
The other eight draws are green. \\
$P=9(0.04)(0.96)^8(0.04)$ \\
$=9(0.96)^8(0.04)^2$ \\
$=0.0103880099\ldots\approx0.0104$. \\
(c) Let $D$ count draws to the $n$th yellow; each yellow has probability $p$. \\
For a negative binomial count, $E(D)=\frac{n}{p}$. \\
The variance is $\mathrm{Var}(D)=\frac{n(1-p)}{p^2}$. \\
The given standard deviation is $300$, so variance $=300^2=90000$. \\
Divide variance by mean to remove $n$: \\
$\frac{\mathrm{Var}(D)}{E(D)}=\frac{1-p}{p}$. \\
$\frac{90000}{22500}=\frac{1-p}{p}$. \\
$4=\frac{1-p}{p}$. \\
Multiply by $p$: $4p=1-p$. \\
Add $p$: $5p=1$. \\
$p=0.2$. \\
(d) Let $p$ be bag C's yellow proportion. \\
Use $H_0:p=0.04$ and $H_1:p<0.04$. \\
Under $H_0$, $J$ has a geometric distribution with $p=0.04$. \\
Smaller $p$ makes longer waits more likely, so use $J\geq c$. \\
The upper-tail probability is $P(J\geq c)=(1-p)^{c-1}$. \\
Under $H_0$, this is $(0.96)^{c-1}$. \\
Set $(0.96)^{c-1}<0.05$. \\
Take logs: $(c-1)\log(0.96)<\log(0.05)$. \\
Since $\log(0.96)<0$, dividing reverses the inequality: \\
$c-1>\frac{\log(0.05)}{\log(0.96)}=73.38525\ldots$. \\
Hence $c>74.38525\ldots$ and the smallest integer is $75$. \\
Check: $P(J\geq74)=0.96^{73}=0.0507925\ldots>0.05$. \\
$P(J\geq75)=0.96^{74}=0.0487608\ldots<0.05$. \\
The critical region is $J\geq75$. \\
(e) Tom observed $J=24$, which is below $75$. \\
Do not reject $H_0$; there is insufficient evidence at 5% of a smaller yellow proportion in bag C. \\
(f) At the true bag C proportion $p=0.013$, power is the probability of the critical region. \\
$P(J\geq75)=(1-0.013)^{75-1}$ \\
$=(0.987)^{74}$ \\
$=0.379725093\ldots$ \\
$=0.380$ to 3 significant figures, as required.

@topic Generating functions under transformations
@marks 14
@question
The probability generating function of the random variable $X$ is $G_X(t)=k(2+t)^4$ where $k$ is a constant. \\
(a) Show that $k=\frac{1}{81}$. (2) \\
(b) Find $P(X=2)$. (2) \\
(c) Find the probability generating function of $W=3X+2$. (2) \\
The probability generating function of the random variable $Y$ is $G_Y(t)=\frac{t(2+t)^2}{9}$. Given that $X$ and $Y$ are independent, \\
(d) find the probability generating function of $U=X+Y$ in its simplest form. (2) \\
(e) Use calculus to find the value of $\mathrm{Var}(U)$. (6)
@scheme
(a) $G_X(1)=1$ gives $k(2+1)^4=81k=1$. M1 2.1 \\
$k=\frac{1}{81}$. A1* 1.1b (2) \\
(b) $P(X=2)$ is the coefficient of $t^2$ in $\frac{(2+t)^4}{81}$. M1 1.1b \\
$P(X=2)=\frac{4C2(2)^2}{81}=\frac{8}{27}$. A1 1.1b (2) \\
(c) $G_W(t)=E(t^{3X+2})=t^2G_X(t^3)$. M1 3.1a \\
$=\frac{t^2(2+t^3)^4}{81}$. A1 1.1b (2) \\
(d) By independence, $G_U(t)=G_X(t)G_Y(t)$. M1 3.1a \\
$=\frac{t(2+t)^6}{729}$. A1 1.1b (2) \\
(e) $G'_U(t)=\frac{(2+t)^6+6t(2+t)^5}{729}$. M1 2.1 \\
$G'_U(1)=3$. A1ft 1.1b \\
$G''_U(t)=\frac{12(2+t)^5+30t(2+t)^4}{729}$. M1 2.1 \\
$G''_U(1)=\frac{22}{3}$. A1 1.1b \\
$\mathrm{Var}(U)=G''_U(1)+G'_U(1)-[G'_U(1)]^2$. M1 2.1 \\
$=\frac{22}{3}+3-9=\frac{4}{3}$. A1 1.1b (6) \\
Note: The supplied shadow scheme contains stray unrelated variance lines after the correct $\frac{4}{3}$ result. The generating function above independently gives $\frac{4}{3}$.
@model
(a) A probability generating function has value $1$ at $t=1$. \\
$G_X(1)=k(2+1)^4=1$. \\
$81k=1$. \\
Divide by $81$: $k=\frac{1}{81}$, as required. \\
(b) Expand with the binomial theorem: \\
$(2+t)^4=2^4+4(2^3)t+6(2^2)t^2+4(2)t^3+t^4$. \\
$=16+32t+24t^2+8t^3+t^4$. \\
Divide by $81$: $G_X(t)=\frac{16+32t+24t^2+8t^3+t^4}{81}$. \\
The coefficient of $t^2$ is $P(X=2)=\frac{24}{81}=\frac{8}{27}$. \\
(c) Use $W=3X+2$. \\
$G_W(t)=E(t^W)=E(t^{3X+2})$. \\
$=t^2E((t^3)^X)$ \\
$=t^2G_X(t^3)$ \\
$=\frac{t^2(2+t^3)^4}{81}$. \\
(d) For independent $X$ and $Y$, the generating function of their sum is the product. \\
$G_U(t)=G_X(t)G_Y(t)$ \\
$=\frac{(2+t)^4}{81}\frac{t(2+t)^2}{9}$ \\
$=\frac{t(2+t)^6}{729}$. \\
(e) Use the product rule on $G_U(t)=\frac{t(2+t)^6}{729}$. \\
$G'_U(t)=\frac{(2+t)^6}{729}+\frac{6t(2+t)^5}{729}$. \\
At $t=1$, $G'_U(1)=\frac{3^6+6(3^5)}{729}$. \\
$=\frac{729+1458}{729}=3$. \\
Differentiate each term again: \\
$G''_U(t)=\frac{6(2+t)^5}{729}+\frac{6(2+t)^5+30t(2+t)^4}{729}$. \\
$=\frac{12(2+t)^5+30t(2+t)^4}{729}$. \\
At $t=1$, $G''_U(1)=\frac{12(3^5)+30(3^4)}{729}$. \\
$=\frac{2916+2430}{729}$ \\
$=\frac{5346}{729}=\frac{22}{3}$. \\
Use $G''_U(1)=E[U(U-1)]$ and $G'_U(1)=E(U)$. \\
$\mathrm{Var}(U)=G''_U(1)+G'_U(1)-[G'_U(1)]^2$. \\
$=\frac{22}{3}+3-3^2$ \\
$=\frac{22}{3}-6$ \\
$=\frac{4}{3}$.

@topic Normal test sample size and Type II
@marks 8
@question
A manufacturer has a machine that produces jars of jam. The volume of jam produced by the machine is normally distributed with unknown mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $0.15$. Andrew believes that the machine is not working properly and the mean volume of the jars of jam has decreased. He takes a random sample of size $n$ to test, at the 1% level of significance, the hypotheses $H_0:\mu=22$ and $H_1:\mu<22$. \\
(a) Write down the size of this test. (1) \\
Given that the actual value of $\mu$ is $21.8$, \\
(b)(i) calculate the minimum value of $n$ such that the probability of a Type II error is less than $0.05$. Show your working clearly. (6) \\
(ii) Andrew uses the same sample size, $n$, but now carries out the test at a 5% level of significance. Without doing any further calculations, state how this would affect the probability of a Type II error. (1)
@scheme
(a) Size of test $=0.01$. B1 1.2 (1) \\
(b)(i) Let the rejection region be sample mean $<k$. Under $H_0$, $\frac{k-22}{0.15/\sqrt{n}}=-2.32635\ldots$. M1 3.4 \\
Thus $k=22-\frac{0.348952\ldots}{\sqrt{n}}$. A1 1.1b \\
For true mean $21.8$, Type II error $=P(\text{sample mean}\geq k)<0.05$. M1 3.4 \\
This requires $\frac{k-21.8}{0.15/\sqrt{n}}>1.64485\ldots$. A1ft 1.1b \\
Substitution gives $0.2\sqrt{n}>0.15(2.32635+1.64485)$, so $\sqrt{n}>2.9784\ldots$. M1 1.1b \\
The minimum integer is $n=9$. A1 2.1 (6) \\
(ii) Increasing the significance level to 5% decreases the Type II error probability. B1 2.2a (1)
@model
(a) Test size is the probability of a Type I error under $H_0$. \\
At a 1% significance level, the size is $0.01$. \\
(b)(i) Let the critical rule be sample mean $<k$. \\
For size $0.01$ under $H_0:\mu=22$, use the lower standard normal quantile $z=-2.3263479\ldots$. \\
$\frac{k-22}{0.15/\sqrt{n}}=-2.3263479\ldots$. \\
Multiply by $0.15/\sqrt{n}$: \\
$k-22=-\frac{0.3489522\ldots}{\sqrt{n}}$. \\
$k=22-\frac{0.3489522\ldots}{\sqrt{n}}$. \\
At the true mean $21.8$, Type II error means the sample mean is at least $k$. \\
To make that upper-tail probability less than $0.05$, require \\
$\frac{k-21.8}{0.15/\sqrt{n}}>1.6448536\ldots$. \\
Substitute the expression for $k$: \\
$\frac{0.2-0.3489522\ldots/\sqrt{n}}{0.15/\sqrt{n}}>1.6448536\ldots$. \\
Multiply by $0.15$: $0.2\sqrt{n}-0.3489522\ldots>0.2467280\ldots$. \\
Add $0.3489522\ldots$: $0.2\sqrt{n}>0.5956802\ldots$. \\
Divide by $0.2$: $\sqrt{n}>2.978401\ldots$. \\
Square: $n>8.870873\ldots$. \\
The minimum integer is $n=9$. \\
Check: Type II probability at $n=8$ is $0.07424\ldots$, and at $n=9$ it is $0.04710\ldots<0.05$. \\
(ii) With a 5% significance level, the rejection region grows for the same $n$. \\
Failing to reject at true mean $21.8$ becomes less likely, so the Type II error probability decreases.
