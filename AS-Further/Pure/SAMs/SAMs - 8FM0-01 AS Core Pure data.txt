@question
$f(z)=z^{3}+pz^{2}+qz-15$ where $p$ and $q$ are real constants. \\
Given that the equation $f(z)=0$ has roots $\alpha$, $\frac{5}{\alpha}$ and $\left(\alpha+\frac{5}{\alpha}-1\right)$ \\
(a) solve completely the equation $f(z)=0$ (5) \\
(b) Hence find the value of $p$. (2)
@scheme
(a) $\alpha\left(\frac{5}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha+\frac{5}{\alpha}-1\right)=15$ M1 1.1b \\
$5\alpha+\frac{25}{\alpha}-5=15$ A1 1.1b \\
$\alpha^{2}-4\alpha+5=0$ M1 3.1a \\
$\alpha=2\pm\mathrm{i}$ A1 1.1b \\

$p=-7$ A1 1.1b (2) \\
Notes: \\
(a) M1: Multiplies the three given roots together and sets the result equal to 15 or -15. \\
A1: Obtains a correct equation in $\alpha$. \\
M1: Forms a quadratic equation in $\alpha$ and attempts to solve. \\
A1: $\alpha=2\pm\mathrm{i}$. \\
A1: Deduces the three roots. \\
(b) M1: Uses the sum of the roots or expands the factors. \\

@model
Use the product of roots of a monic cubic: $-(-15)=15$. \\
$\alpha\left(\frac{5}{\alpha}\right)\left(\alpha+\frac{5}{\alpha}-1\right)=15$ \\
Cancel $\alpha$ in the first two factors: \\
$5\left(\alpha+\frac{5}{\alpha}-1\right)=15$ \\

$(\alpha-2)^{2}=-1$ \\
$\alpha=2\pm\mathrm{i}$ \\

(a) $z=2+\mathrm{i},\ 2-\mathrm{i},\ 3$ \\
(b) Use the sum of roots $=-p$. \\
$-p=(2+\mathrm{i})+(2-\mathrm{i})+3$ \\

@question
The cubic equation $x^{3}+3x^{2}-8x+6=0$ has roots $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$. \\
Without solving the equation, find the cubic equation whose roots are $(\alpha-1)$, $(\beta-1)$ and $(\gamma-1)$, giving your answer in the form $w^{3}+pw^{2}+qw+r=0$, where $p$, $q$ and $r$ are integers to be found. (5)
@scheme
Let $w=x-1$, so $x=w+1$. B1 3.1a \\
$(w+1)^{3}+3(w+1)^{2}-8(w+1)+6=0$ M1 3.1a \\
$w^{3}+3w^{2}+3w+1+3(w^{2}+2w+1)-8w-8+6=0$ M1 1.1b \\
$w^{3}+6w^{2}+w+2=0$ A1 1.1b, A1 1.1b (5) \\
Notes: \\
B1: Relates $x$ and $w$. \\
M1: Substitutes into the original polynomial. \\
M1: Correct expansion. \\
A1: Correct three coefficients. \\
A1: Correct equation in the requested form.
@model
Let $w=x-1$ so a root $x=\alpha$ gives $w=\alpha-1$, and similarly for the other roots. \\
Rearrange: $x=w+1$. \\
Substitute into the original equation: \\
$(w+1)^{3}+3(w+1)^{2}-8(w+1)+6=0$ \\
Expand the cube: $(w+1)^{3}=w^{3}+3w^{2}+3w+1$. \\
Expand the square: $(w+1)^{2}=w^{2}+2w+1$. \\
$w^{3}+3w^{2}+3w+1+3w^{2}+6w+3-8w-8+6=0$ \\
Collect the $w^{2}$ terms: $3w^{2}+3w^{2}=6w^{2}$. \\
Collect the $w$ terms: $3w+6w-8w=w$. \\
Collect constants: $1+3-8+6=2$. \\
$w^{3}+6w^{2}+w+2=0$ \\
$p=6$, $q=1$, $r=2$.

@topic Matrix determinant and rotation
@marks 7
@question
$M$ has rows $(1,-\sqrt{3})$; $(\sqrt{3},1)$. \\
(a) Show that $M$ is non-singular. (2) \\
The hexagon $R$ is transformed to the hexagon $S$ by the transformation represented by the matrix $M$. \\
Given that the area of hexagon $R$ is 5 square units, \\
(b) find the area of hexagon $S$. (1) \\
The matrix $M$ represents an enlargement, with centre $(0,0)$ and scale factor $k$, where $k>0$, followed by a rotation anti-clockwise through an angle $\theta$ about $(0,0)$. \\
(c) Find the value of $k$. (2) \\
(d) Find the value of $\theta$. (2)
@scheme
(a) $\mathrm{det}(M)=(1)(1)-(\sqrt{3})(-\sqrt{3})$ M1 1.1a \\
$\mathrm{det}(M)=4\neq0$ so $M$ is non-singular A1 2.4 (2) \\
(b) $\mathrm{Area}(S)=4(5)=20$ B1ft 1.2 (1) \\
(c) $k=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}(M)}$ M1 1.1b \\
$k=2$ A1ft 1.1b (2) \\
(d) $\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}$ or $\sin\theta=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ M1 1.1b \\
$\theta=60$ degrees or $\frac{\pi}{3}$ A1 1.1b (2) \\
Notes: \\
(a) M1: Attempts to find the determinant. \\
A1: $\mathrm{det}(M)=4$ and a conclusion based on nonzero determinant. \\
(b) B1ft: Area scale factor is their determinant. \\
(c) M1: Uses $k=\sqrt{\mathrm{det}(M)}$. \\
A1ft: $k=2$. \\
(d) M1: Uses a matrix entry to find a trigonometric ratio. \\
A1: $60$ degrees or $\frac{\pi}{3}$.
@model
(a) Use the determinant formula $ad-bc$. \\
$\mathrm{det}(M)=1(1)-(-\sqrt{3})(\sqrt{3})$ \\
$=1-(-3)$ \\
$=4$ \\
Since $4\neq0$, $M$ is non-singular. \\
(b) Areas scale by the absolute determinant. \\
$\mathrm{Area}(S)=|4|\times5$ \\
$=20$ square units. \\
(c) An enlargement by $k$ scales area by $k^{2}$; a rotation preserves area. \\
$k^{2}=4$ \\
Since $k>0$, $k=2$. \\

$2\cos\theta=1$ \\

@topic Induction and summation
@marks 15
@question
(a) Prove by induction that for all positive integers $n$, $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^{2}=\frac{1}{6}n(n+1)(2n+1)$. (6) \\
(b) Use the standard results for $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^{3}$ and $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r$ to show that for all positive integers $n$, $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r(r+6)(r-6)=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n-8)(n+9)$. (4) \\
(c) Hence find the value of $n$ that satisfies $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r(r+6)(r-6)=17\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^{2}$. (5)
@scheme
(a) For $n=1$, both sides equal 1. B1 2.2a \\
Assume $\sum_{r=1}^{k}r^{2}=\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(2k+1)$. M1 2.4 \\
$\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}r^{2}=\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(2k+1)+(k+1)^{2}$ M1 2.1 \\
$=\frac{1}{6}(k+1)(2k^{2}+7k+6)$ A1 1.1b \\
$=\frac{1}{6}(k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)$ A1 1.1b \\
This is the required result for $k+1$; as it holds for 1, it holds for all positive integers. A1 2.4 (6) \\
(b) $\sum_{r=1}^{n}r(r+6)(r-6)=\sum_{r=1}^{n}(r^{3}-36r)$ \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}(n+1)^{2}-18n(n+1)$ M1 2.1, A1 1.1b \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)[n(n+1)-72]$ M1 1.1b \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n-8)(n+9)$ A1* 1.1b (4) \\
(c) $\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n-8)(n+9)=\frac{17}{6}n(n+1)(2n+1)$ M1 3.1a \\
$3(n-8)(n+9)=34(2n+1)$ M1 1.1b \\
$3n^{2}-65n-250=0$ A1 1.1b \\
Solve the quadratic. M1 1.1b \\
$n=25$ is the positive integer solution. A1 1.1b (5) \\
Notes: \\
(a) B1: Checks the base case on both sides. \\
M1: Assumes the formula for $n=k$. \\
M1: Adds $(k+1)^{2}$. \\
A1: Correct expansion. \\
A1: Correct factorisation for $k+1$. \\
A1: Complete induction conclusion. \\
(b) M1: Uses a standard summation formula. \\
A1: Correct expression. \\
M1: Factors $n(n+1)$. \\
A1*: Obtains the given result. \\
(c) M1: Equates the results. \\
M1: Cancels $n(n+1)$. \\
A1: Correct quadratic. \\
M1: Valid solution method. \\
A1: Only $n=25$.
@model
(a) Use mathematical induction. \\
Base case $n=1$: $\sum_{r=1}^{1}r^{2}=1$. \\
The formula gives $\frac{1}{6}(1)(2)(3)=1$. \\
Assume for $n=k$ that $\sum_{r=1}^{k}r^{2}=\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(2k+1)$. \\
For $n=k+1$, add the next term: \\
$\sum_{r=1}^{k+1}r^{2}=\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(2k+1)+(k+1)^{2}$ \\
Factor $k+1$: \\
$=\frac{1}{6}(k+1)[k(2k+1)+6(k+1)]$ \\
Expand inside the bracket: \\
$=\frac{1}{6}(k+1)(2k^{2}+7k+6)$ \\
Factor the quadratic: \\
$=\frac{1}{6}(k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)$ \\
Since $2k+3=2(k+1)+1$, this is the formula for $k+1$. \\
The base case and induction step prove the result for all positive integers. \\
(b) Expand the summand: \\
$r(r+6)(r-6)=r(r^{2}-36)$ \\
$=r^{3}-36r$ \\
Use $\sum r^{3}=[\frac{n(n+1)}{2}]^{2}$ and $\sum r=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. \\
$\sum_{r=1}^{n}(r^{3}-36r)=\frac{n^{2}(n+1)^{2}}{4}-18n(n+1)$ \\
Factor out $\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)$: \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)[n(n+1)-72]$ \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n^{2}+n-72)$ \\
$=\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n-8)(n+9)$ as required. \\
(c) Substitute the two results into the equation: \\
$\frac{1}{4}n(n+1)(n-8)(n+9)=\frac{17}{6}n(n+1)(2n+1)$ \\
As $n$ is positive, $n(n+1)\neq0$; cancel it. \\
$\frac{1}{4}(n-8)(n+9)=\frac{17}{6}(2n+1)$ \\
Multiply by 12: $3(n-8)(n+9)=34(2n+1)$. \\
Expand: $3(n^{2}+n-72)=68n+34$. \\
$3n^{2}+3n-216=68n+34$ \\
$3n^{2}-65n-250=0$ \\
Factor: $(3n+10)(n-25)=0$. \\
$n=-\frac{10}{3}$ or $n=25$. \\
Reject the negative, noninteger root. \\
$n=25$.

$0.16=0.04k$ \\
$k=4$ \\

@question
An octopus is able to catch any fish that swim within a distance of 2 m from the octopus's position. \\
A fish $F$ swims from a point $A$ to a point $B$. \\
The octopus is modelled as a fixed particle at the origin $O$. \\
Fish $F$ is modelled as a particle moving in a straight line from $A$ to $B$. \\
Relative to $O$, the coordinates of $A$ are $(-3,1,-7)$ and the coordinates of $B$ are $(9,4,11)$, where the unit of distance is metres. \\
(a) Use the model to determine whether or not the octopus is able to catch fish $F$. (7) \\
(b) Criticise the model in relation to fish $F$. (1) \\
(c) Criticise the model in relation to the octopus. (1)
@scheme
